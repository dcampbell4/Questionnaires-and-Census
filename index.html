The Most Boring Superpower on Earth

Why a statistical agency addresses its mail to a building rather than a person, and what happens to a national picture when only 61.3% of households answer. Introduces survey weighting as a judgment call.

Guiding questions
  • If two official poverty measures describe the same people in the same year, in what sense is either one true?
  • Weighting corrects for households the survey could not reach. What has to be assumed about them, and who decides?

The Science of Measuring People

What a personality test and a national census have in common: both have to operationalise something they cannot measure directly, then force messy human answers into categories. Covers informants, non-response bias, weighting, and why the data is not the world.

Guiding questions
  • If "employed" can mean forty hours or two, what exactly does an employment rate tell you?
  • Categories are useful because they simplify reality. What is lost, and when does the loss matter?
  • How much does the observer affect the observation when one person answers for a whole household?

Fragile Statistics: the illusion of certainty

The deck we work through in class, comparing the US CPS ASEC and Brazil's Continuous PNAD. It traces one headline figure back through its definitions, its 61.3% weighted response rate and the adjustments made to repair the sample, ending on the claim that statistics are constructed rather than discovered.

Guiding questions
  • Both surveys depend entirely on voluntary cooperation. What does that make of the knowledge they produce?
  • Brazil changed its visit rule to salvage the data through Covid-19. Can a time series survive its own methodology changing?
  • If the same country has a 10.2% and a 13.1% poverty rate, what is the poverty rate?
